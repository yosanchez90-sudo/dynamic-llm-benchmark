Benchmark contamination, the presence of evaluation data in a large language model's (LLM) training corpus, inflates reported scores on static test suites. This work presents DyMuT (Dynamic Mutation-based Testing), a benchmark for LLM unit-test generation, and tests whether it resists this inflation better than a state-of-the-art static alternative. Sixty parameterised templates spanning four cyclomatic-complexity tiers generate fresh, seeded instances at evaluation time, scored by a seven-operator mutation-testing suite rather than fixed tests. Nine models were evaluated on DyMuT and on a decontaminated static baseline (ULT). A small open-source model was then fine-tuned on ULT's ground truth to test how each benchmark responds to contamination. The fine-tuned model gained \HTwoSeenGain{} percentage points on the memorised static split, against \HTwoLOneLThreeGain{} points on a control that measures general improvement. Its DyMuT score did not move at all. DyMuT rejects the contamination that the static benchmark absorbs. Six hypotheses were pre-specified. Where p-values apply, they are corrected with the Holm-Bonferroni procedure. The causal evidence for contamination resistance holds. One pre-specified prediction was contradicted. DyMuT tracked external code-generation ability more closely than the static benchmark did. One comparison was not distinguishable from no effect, and one stability threshold was not met. The findings support dynamic, template-based generation as a workable way to evaluate LLM test generation without contamination inflating the result.
