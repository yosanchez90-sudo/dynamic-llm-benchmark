\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results come from two kinds of test, one causal and one observational. H2's causal result shows contamination as a training-time memorisation effect: the fine-tuned checkpoint reproduces its training targets verbatim and passes near-perfectly on the exact problems it trained on. H1's observational results are weaker, and ask a different question: do models trained by others, on corpora nobody here can inspect, already carry this effect for ULT's published content? The two do not conflict; a causal probe with one controlled intervention can show a large, clear effect that an observational comparison across nine models with unknown training histories cannot be expected to recover as clearly.

One further finding is worth stating. After fine-tuning, the mutation score on the seen-ULT split is $\HTwoPostMutk{}$, not \MutkCeiling{}, even though the model reproduces its training target word for word. Byte-identical recall of a reference test suite does not guarantee the recalled suite kills every mutant the scoring harness generates. Memorisation alone can only inflate a mutation-testing score so far, whether or not the benchmark resists contamination.

The benchmark's validity argument is top-down by design: complexity tiers, mutation operators, and scoring metric are all specified in advance from an explicit model of what test-generation capability should look like. H6 supplies the complementary bottom-up check: does the benchmark's empirical ranking of models agree with an independent capability measure? The answer is mixed: the conditional primary metric lands inside the pre-registered validity band, though it does not reach significance at this sample size, while the coverage-adjusted co-primary exceeds it, mostly reflecting executability rather than mutation-testing quality. Neither reading settles the question alone; together they place DyMuT close to its intended target.

Several threats to validity apply. Running untrusted code in a subprocess is not the same as running it safely: an early ground-truth quality check executed thousands of real-world ULT functions with the working directory left at the project root, and several wrote files as a side effect of ordinary relative-path I/O, before the check was moved to an isolated temporary directory. The causal fine-tuning result (H2) uses a single model at a single, small parameter scale; whether the same clean separation between memorisation and transfer holds at larger scale is untested here. Mutation score is conditional on baseline correctness throughout, which Section~\ref{sec:results} shows affects cross-benchmark rank agreement in H6, a property of the metric definition, not of the benchmark itself.

\subsection{ULT's Internal Consistency as a Ground-Truth Reference}
\label{sec:discussion-ult}
Preparing ULT for fine-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:methods-finetune}) surfaced findings about ULT itself, reported factually as recomputations of a published artefact's own stated properties, not as criticism. Only a small minority of ULT's shipped test-and-code pairs pass this study's harness unmodified. ULT's self-reported complexity field disagrees with an independent AST-based recompute at exactly the CC$\geq$8 boundary this study filters on: 233 of its 3,909 records the vendor's metadata claims exceed that floor do not. This study filters on its own recomputation throughout, so no result here depends on the discrepancy. Independently, ULT's source paper's stated mutation-score formula contradicts its own published scoring code: this study's co-primary (coverage-adjusted) definition matches the paper's prose, while the primary (conditional) definition matches its code, which is why both are reported as co-primaries rather than one being canonical. Neither discrepancy affects this study's own metrics, since both are disclosed and accounted for in how ULT is loaded and scored.
