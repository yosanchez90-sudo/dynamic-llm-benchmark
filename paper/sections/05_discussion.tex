\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results come from two kinds of test, one causal and one observational. H2's causal result shows contamination as a training-time memorisation effect: the fine-tuned checkpoint reproduces its training targets verbatim and passes near-perfectly on the exact problems it trained on. H1's observational results are weaker, and they ask a different question. Do models trained by others, on corpora nobody here can inspect, already carry this effect for ULT's published content? The two results do not conflict. A causal probe with one controlled intervention can show a large, clear effect. An observational comparison across nine models with unknown training histories cannot be expected to recover the same effect as clearly.

One further finding is worth stating. After fine-tuning, the mutation score on the seen-ULT split is $\HTwoPostMutk{}$, not \MutkCeiling{}, even though the model reproduces its training target word for word. Byte-identical recall of a reference test suite does not guarantee the recalled suite kills every mutant the scoring harness generates. Memorisation alone can only inflate a mutation-testing score so far, whether or not the benchmark resists contamination.

The benchmark's validity argument is top-down by design: complexity tiers, mutation operators, and scoring metric are all specified in advance from an explicit model of what test-generation capability should look like. H6 supplies the complementary bottom-up check: Does the benchmark's empirical ranking of models agree with an independent capability measure? The answer is mixed. The conditional primary metric lands inside the pre-registered validity band, though it does not reach significance at this sample size. The coverage-adjusted co-primary exceeds the band. Breaking that figure down shows most of its agreement is about executability, not about mutation-testing quality. Neither reading settles the question alone. Together they place DyMuT close to its intended target, correlated with general code-generation ability.

Several threats to validity apply. Running untrusted code in a subprocess is not the same as running it safely. An early ground-truth quality check executed thousands of real-world ULT functions with the working directory left at the project root. Several of those functions wrote files as a side effect of ordinary relative-path I/O. The check now runs from an isolated temporary directory. The causal fine-tuning result (H2) uses a single model at a single, small parameter scale; whether the same clean separation between memorisation and transfer holds at larger scale is untested here. Mutation score is conditional on baseline correctness throughout. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows this affects cross-benchmark rank agreement in H6. It is a property of the metric definition, not of the benchmark itself.

\subsection{ULT's Internal Consistency as a Ground-Truth Reference}
\label{sec:discussion-ult}
Preparing ULT for fine-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:methods-finetune}) surfaced findings about ULT itself, reported here factually and neutrally, as recomputations of a published artefact's own stated properties rather than as a criticism of it. First, only a small minority of ULT's shipped test-and-code pairs pass this study's own scoring harness unmodified; most required a repair before they could be used as fine-tuning targets. Second, ULT's self-reported cyclomatic-complexity field disagrees with an independent, AST-based recomputation at exactly the boundary that determines whether a record clears this study's CC$\geq$8 filter: 233 of ULT's 3,909 records that the vendor's own metadata claims exceed its stated complexity floor do not, under this study's first-party recompute. This study filters on its own recomputation throughout, never on the vendor's claimed field, so no result in this paper depends on the discrepancy; it is reported as a caveat on ULT's suitability as a ground-truth reference for other work, not as a defect in this study's own measurements. Third, and independently, ULT's source paper's stated mutation-score formula contradicts its own published scoring code: this study's co-primary (coverage-adjusted) definition matches the source paper's prose formula, while this study's primary (conditional) definition matches the paper's own published code instead, which is why both are reported as co-primaries throughout Section~\ref{sec:results} rather than one being treated as canonical. None of this study's own reported metrics are affected by either discrepancy, since both are disclosed and accounted for in how ULT is loaded and scored.
