\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The pattern across H1--H6 is most coherently read as evidence from two distinct kinds of test -- one causal, one observational -- rather than as a single, uniform contamination effect. H2's causal result shows contamination as a training-time memorisation effect: the fine-tuned checkpoint reproduces its training targets verbatim and passes near-perfectly on the exact problems it trained on, while transferring almost nothing to problems it did not see, whether those problems are on the dynamic benchmark or on ULT's own held-out, unseen split. H1's observational results, weaker and more equivocal by design, probe a different, standing question -- whether models trained by others, on unknown corpora, already carry this same effect for ULT's specific published content. The two results are not in tension: a causal probe with a single, controlled intervention can show a clean, large effect that an observational comparison across nine models of unknown training history cannot be expected to recover with the same clarity.

A further finding shows what contamination could have bought even had the dynamic benchmark been susceptible: post-fine-tuning mutation score on the seen-ULT split, despite 100\% verbatim reproduction of the training target, is $\HTwoPostMutk{}$, not 1.0. Byte-identical recall of a reference test suite does not guarantee the recalled suite kills every mutant the scoring harness generates, since mutation testing scores test quality against a mutant population the training data itself never specified. This sets a ceiling on how much a purely memorisation-driven strategy can inflate a mutation-testing score, independent of whether the underlying benchmark resists contamination at all.

H6's construct-validity result is best read through a top-down/bottom-up framing rather than as a single correlation coefficient. The benchmark's validity argument is top-down by design: complexity tiers, mutation operators, and scoring metric are all specified in advance from an explicit model of what test-generation capability should look like, independent of any external comparison. H6 supplies the complementary bottom-up check: does the benchmark's empirical ranking of models agree with an independent capability measure? The answer is genuinely mixed -- the conditional primary metric's point estimate lands inside the pre-registered validity band, though not significantly so at the available sample size, while the coverage-adjusted co-primary exceeds it, decomposing to reveal that most of the latter agreement is really agreement about executability rather than about mutation-testing quality specifically. Neither reading alone settles the question; together they place the benchmark closer to its intended target -- correlated with general code-generation capability, without being reducible to it -- than either extreme would.

Several threats to validity apply. Executing untrusted, template-generated or scraped ground-truth code in a subprocess is not equivalent to sandboxing it: an early ground-truth quality check that executed thousands of real-world ULT functions with the working directory left at the project root produced dozens of stray files from functions performing ordinary relative-path I/O as a side effect, a lesson since fixed by always executing untrusted code from an isolated temporary directory but retained here as a caution about the gap between "runs in a subprocess" and "runs safely." The dynamic benchmark's problems are template-generated rather than drawn from real-world repositories, trading contamination resistance for a synthetic quality that may not fully represent the structural variety of production code, an explicit design choice rather than an oversight but one that limits how far the results generalise to naturalistic code. The causal fine-tuning result (H2) uses a single model at a single, small parameter scale; whether the same clean separation between memorisation and transfer holds at larger scale is untested here. Mutation score throughout is conditional on baseline correctness by design (Section~\ref{sec:methods-metrics}), which Section~\ref{sec:results} shows materially affects cross-benchmark rank agreement (H6) and should be read as a property of the specific metric definition rather than of the benchmark's underlying validity. ULT's own published ground truth passes this study's harness at a substantially lower rate than its role as a correctness reference would suggest, a finding independently corroborated by an internal recomputation of the benchmark's own complexity labels and by a direct contradiction between the source paper's stated mutation-score formula and its own published scoring code: this study's co-primary (coverage-adjusted) definition matches the source paper's prose formula, while this study's primary (conditional) definition matches the paper's own published code instead, which is why both are reported as co-primaries throughout rather than either being singled out as "the" literature-matching score. None of this study's own reported metrics are silently affected by the contradiction, since it is disclosed and accounted for rather than resolved by picking a side; the finding is reported here as a caveat on ULT's suitability as a ground-truth reference more broadly.
